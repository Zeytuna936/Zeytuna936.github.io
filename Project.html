Aim of the Project

Armed conflict can shape development long after active hostilities end. Its effects can operate through damaged productive capacity and infrastructure, weaker institutions, disrupted investment and trade, labour-market dislocation, and migration. Together, these channels can influence both the pace and the composition of post-conflict recovery.

The Yugoslav Wars provide a comparative setting in which these long-run effects can be examined across countries that emerged from the same former federation but experienced very different levels of conflict exposure. Bosnia and Herzegovina, Croatia, and Serbia were directly affected by major hostilities, while Montenegro, North Macedonia, and Slovenia followed comparatively less violent paths to independence.

This project examines how development outcomes have diverged across the six former Yugoslav republics, with particular attention to the countries most directly affected by conflict. It compares selected indicators of economic performance, institutional quality, and social development to identify patterns in post-conflict recovery. The analysis is descriptive rather than causal: its purpose is to surface cross-country differences and plausible long-run relationships, not to attribute present-day outcomes to conflict alone.

Data Limitations

Cross-country comparability is constrained by uneven data coverage across both countries and years. Missing observations are particularly relevant for Bosnia and Herzegovina (BH), North Macedonia (NM), and Montenegro, including within the emissions series presented in Chart 5. Historical country-level data prior to the breakup of Yugoslavia are also limited because many indicators were reported for Yugoslavia as a single state; consequently, a consistent pre-conflict baseline cannot be constructed for several measures, including the 1990 observations relevant to Chart 2.

For Charts 3 and 6, no single official or high-reliability source provided fully comparable coverage for all six countries. Supplementary values from Wikipedia were therefore used to address selected gaps. Because the source contained multiple tables with inconsistent labels and section structures, the initial Python extraction produced mixed outputs. The resulting datasets were manually reviewed and standardised in Excel before visualisation. These source and harmonisation constraints mean the charts should be interpreted as comparative descriptive evidence rather than as a fully harmonised dataset suitable for causal inference.

Conclusion

The visual evidence points to substantial divergence in development trajectories across the former Yugoslav republics. Slovenia and Croatia generally record stronger outcomes across the economic, social, and institutional indicators presented. Serbia combines relatively high aggregate GDP with weaker performance on several labour-market and governance measures, while Bosnia and Herzegovina appears more constrained across both economic and institutional dimensions. North Macedonia and Montenegro display outcomes that also reflect structural factors such as geography, market size, and sectoral composition.

These patterns are consistent with the view that conflict legacy may remain relevant to long-run development, but the analysis does not establish that conflict alone caused the observed differences. Institutional reform, economic structure, international integration, geography, and other country-specific factors also shape recovery trajectories. The central comparative finding is therefore one of uneven recovery: the countries emerging from the same former federation have followed markedly different development paths, with Bosnia and Herzegovina showing the clearest combination of persistent economic and institutional constraints among the indicators examined here.
